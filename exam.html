Social Area

4 studies · 2 themes · Authority & helping behaviour

Milgram (1963) — Obedience
SocialClassicTheme: Responses to authority
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether ordinary Americans would obey an authority figure who instructed them to harm an innocent person
  • To test the situational explanation for obedience
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment; independent measures
  • Quantitative data: % who obeyed at each voltage
  • Standardised procedure — confederate actor played learner
👥 Sample
  • 40 male participants aged 20–50
  • Volunteer sample — responded to newspaper ad
  • All American — New Haven, Connecticut
📋 Procedure
  • Participant assigned "teacher", confederate assigned "learner" (fixed)
  • Learner strapped to chair in adjacent room; teacher administered electric shocks for wrong answers
  • Shocks ranged 15–450V in 15V increments; shock generator labelled up to "XXX Danger"
  • Four verbal prods used by experimenter (e.g. "You must continue")
📊 Results
  • 65% administered the maximum 450V shock
  • All participants continued to 300V
  • Many showed signs of extreme stress (sweating, trembling)
💡 Conclusion
  • Ordinary people will obey authority figures even when instructed to harm others
  • Situational factors (uniform, legitimate setting) override individual conscience
  • Challenges dispositional explanation ("Germans are different")
✓ Strengths
  • Highly controlled — standardised procedure enables replication
  • High mundane realism — participants genuinely believed situation
  • Groundbreaking — changed our understanding of human obedience
✗ Weaknesses
  • Serious ethical issues — deception, psychological harm, right to withdraw unclear
  • Low population validity — all male, American, volunteer
  • Demand characteristics — lab setting may have cued compliance
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Individual vs situational: situational factors (uniform, proximity) determined obedience
  • Ethics: landmark case for ethical guidelines in psychology
  • Usefulness: explains atrocities, police/military obedience
Bocchiaro et al. (2012) — Disobedience and whistleblowing
SocialContemporaryTheme: Responses to authority
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate obedience, disobedience, and whistleblowing when asked to carry out an unethical task by an authority figure
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment — independent measures
  • Comparison with predicted behaviour (prior questionnaire)
  • Three possible responses: comply, disobey, whistleblow
👥 Sample
  • 149 students (78 female, 71 male)
  • VU University Amsterdam — opportunity sample
📋 Procedure
  • Participants asked to write a recruitment letter for an unethical sensory deprivation study on behalf of a fake researcher
  • Could: comply (write letter), disobey (refuse), or whistleblow (report to ethics board via a box in the waiting room)
  • Separate group predicted their own and others' behaviour
📊 Results
  • 76.5% complied — wrote the unethical letter
  • 14.3% disobeyed only
  • 9.4% whistleblew
  • Predictors underestimated compliance and overestimated disobedience
💡 Conclusion
  • Most people comply with authority even when the task is clearly unethical
  • Whistleblowing is extremely rare — individuals overestimate their own moral courage
  • Supports Milgram's findings in a modern, less controversial paradigm
✓ Strengths
  • More ethically acceptable than Milgram — no physical harm
  • Adds whistleblowing as a third response option — extends Milgram
  • Cross-cultural replication — confirms obedience is not culturally unique
✗ Weaknesses
  • Student sample — not representative; reduces population validity
  • Still involves deception — ethical questions remain
  • Writing a letter less extreme than electric shocks — difficult to compare directly
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Individual vs situational: no personality trait predicted compliance; situation drives behaviour
  • Ethnocentrism: cross-cultural design addresses Milgram's American-only sample
Piliavin et al. (1969) — Subway Samaritan
SocialClassicTheme: Responses to people in need
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate the conditions under which bystanders will help a stranger in need, and test whether characteristics of the victim and cost of helping affect bystander intervention
📐 Design
  • Field experiment — natural environment (NY subway)
  • Independent measures: drunk vs ill victim; race of victim
  • DV: % helping, time to help, number of helpers
👥 Sample
  • 4,450 passengers on New York subway (IND 8th Avenue line)
  • Opportunity sample — naturally occurring
  • Mix of races and genders in natural proportions
📋 Procedure
  • Confederate "victim" collapsed in subway carriage after 70 seconds of ride
  • Conditions: victim appeared drunk (smelled of alcohol, carried bottle) OR ill (walked with cane)
  • Race of victim varied: Black or White confederate
  • Observers recorded helping behaviour covertly
📊 Results
  • Ill victim helped 95% of the time (within 70s)
  • Drunk victim helped 50% of the time
  • Race effect: slight in-group preference (own race helped slightly more)
  • Bystander effect less pronounced than expected in enclosed space
💡 Conclusion
  • Helping is influenced by the cost-reward calculation (Arousal: Cost-Reward model)
  • Perceived cause of need (illness vs intoxication) affects likelihood of helping
  • Physical context (no escape) reduces diffusion of responsibility
✓ Strengths
  • High ecological validity — real-world setting, natural behaviour
  • No demand characteristics — participants unaware they were in a study
  • Large sample (4,450 opportunities)
✗ Weaknesses
  • Ethical concerns — participants had no right to withdraw or give consent
  • Low internal validity — many extraneous variables in a field setting
  • Only New York subway — cannot generalise to other cultures/settings
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Individual vs situational: situational cost-reward, not individual values, determined helping
  • Ethics: no consent; participants distressed by witnessing collapse
  • Usefulness: informs understanding of bystander behaviour in emergencies
Levine et al. (2001) — Cross-cultural altruism
SocialContemporaryTheme: Responses to people in need
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether rates of helping behaviour vary across cultures and cities, and what factors predict helping rates
📐 Design
  • Cross-cultural field observation
  • 23 cities in major world countries
  • Three standardised helping scenarios
👥 Sample
  • Passersby in 23 cities across multiple continents
  • Opportunistic — whoever was present
📋 Procedure
  • Scenario 1: dropping pen — would someone pick it up?
  • Scenario 2: limping with injured leg, dropping magazines
  • Scenario 3: blind person needing help crossing road
  • Observers recorded helping behaviour in each city
📊 Results
  • Rio de Janeiro (Brazil) and San José (Costa Rica) highest helping rates
  • Kuala Lumpur and Amsterdam among lowest
  • Overall helping rate 82.5% for blind person scenario
  • Cultural factors (simpatía — value of helpfulness) predicted helping
💡 Conclusion
  • Helping behaviour is culturally variable — not universal
  • Piliavin's model cannot fully explain cross-cultural variation
  • Collectivist cultures (simpatía) show higher helping rates
✓ Strengths
  • Cross-cultural — challenges ethnocentrism of Piliavin's US-only study
  • High ecological validity — natural field setting
  • Large number of cities increases generalisability
✗ Weaknesses
  • No consent — ethical issues remain
  • Situational factors uncontrolled (time of day, weather, crowd density)
  • City-level data doesn't capture individual variation within cultures
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Ethnocentrism: directly addresses Western-centric models of prosocial behaviour
  • Nature vs nurture: cultural variation supports nurture explanation for altruism

Cognitive Area

4 studies · 2 themes · Memory & attention

Loftus & Palmer (1974) — Eyewitness testimony
CognitiveClassicTheme: Memory
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether the wording of a question can alter a witness's memory of an event (post-event information effect)
📐 Design
  • Two experiments — lab; independent measures
  • Exp 1 IV: verb used (smashed/collided/bumped/hit/contacted)
  • Exp 2 IV: smashed vs hit vs no question asked about glass
👥 Sample
  • Exp 1: 45 students (University of Washington)
  • Exp 2: 150 students
  • Both: opportunity samples, American
📋 Procedure
  • Participants watched film clips of car accidents
  • Asked: "About how fast were the cars going when they _____ each other?"
  • Exp 2: one week later asked "Did you see any broken glass?" (no glass in film)
📊 Results
  • Exp 1: "smashed" → mean estimate 40.8 mph; "contacted" → 31.8 mph
  • Exp 2: "smashed" group 32% recalled broken glass; "hit" group 14%; control 12%
💡 Conclusion
  • Post-event questions can distort memory — reconstructive memory
  • Leading questions alter the memory trace itself (substitution hypothesis)
  • Implications for reliability of eyewitness testimony in court
✓ Strengths
  • High control — standardised film clips ensure all Ps saw same accident
  • Cause-and-effect established — verb wording directly changed estimates
  • Hugely applied — changed police interviewing and legal procedures
✗ Weaknesses
  • Low ecological validity — film clips not as emotionally arousing as real accidents
  • Student sample — low population validity
  • Demand characteristics — unusual to estimate speed in a psychology study
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Usefulness: reformed police interview techniques; cognitive interview based on this work
  • Cognitive approach: information processing model — memory as reconstructive, not reproductive
Grant et al. (1998) — Context-dependent memory
CognitiveContemporaryTheme: Memory
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether recall is better when the learning environment matches the recall environment (context-dependent memory)
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment — independent measures
  • 2×2 design: silent/noisy learning × silent/noisy test
  • Four conditions: SS, SN, NS, NN
👥 Sample
  • 39 undergraduate students (psychology)
  • Opportunity sample — one university
📋 Procedure
  • Participants read a two-page article about psychoimmunology
  • Read in either silence or with background café noise
  • Short-answer test and multiple-choice test in either silence or noise
📊 Results
  • Matched conditions (SS and NN) scored significantly higher than mismatched
  • Silent-silent condition had highest scores overall
  • Context match improved both short-answer and MCQ performance
💡 Conclusion
  • Memory is context-dependent — recall is better when context at encoding matches context at retrieval
  • Supports encoding specificity principle
  • Students should revise in conditions similar to exam environment
✓ Strengths
  • Well-controlled — 2×2 design isolates context variable precisely
  • Applied value — directly relevant to how students should revise
✗ Weaknesses
  • Student sample — low population validity
  • Artificial task (psychoimmunology article) — not typical revision material
  • Short-term study — no test of long-term memory effects
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Usefulness: exam revision advice; library vs noisy café revision
  • Cognitive approach: encoding specificity — memory as a cue-dependent retrieval process
Moray (1959) — Dichotic listening / attention
CognitiveClassicTheme: Attention
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate selective attention — specifically, whether unattended messages can break through into conscious awareness, and what determines this
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment — repeated measures across three tasks
  • DV: recall of unattended channel material
👥 Sample
  • Participants from Oxford University
  • Small samples for each experiment (not specified precisely)
  • Opportunity sample
📋 Procedure
  • Participants wore headphones; different messages played to each ear (dichotic listening)
  • Shadowing task: repeat the attended ear message aloud
  • Exp 1: list of words in attended ear repeated 35× — could participants recall from unattended ear?
  • Exp 2: participant's own name presented in unattended channel — detected?
  • Exp 3: instruction to switch ears — followed?
📊 Results
  • Exp 1: rarely noticed repeated words in unattended ear
  • Exp 2: 33% detected own name in unattended channel
  • Exp 3: ~20% followed switch instruction to other ear
💡 Conclusion
  • Most unattended information is filtered out — supports Broadbent's filter theory
  • But personally meaningful stimuli (own name) can break through — affective filter
  • Attention is selective but not completely impermeable
✓ Strengths
  • High control — headphones allow precise delivery of stimuli
  • Multiple experiments testing different aspects of selective attention
✗ Weaknesses
  • Artificial task — dichotic listening not typical real-world experience
  • Small, unspecified samples — reliability concerns
  • Lab setting — demand characteristics possible
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Cognitive approach: filter model of attention; information-processing perspective
  • Usefulness: cocktail party effect; design of warning systems in aviation
Simons & Chabris (1999) — Inattentional blindness
CognitiveContemporaryTheme: Attention
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate inattentional blindness — whether people fail to notice unexpected stimuli when engaged in a demanding visual task
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment — independent measures
  • Variables: easy/hard counting task; transparent/opaque unexpected object; umbrella woman vs gorilla
👥 Sample
  • 228 participants — undergraduate students
  • Harvard University — opportunity sample
📋 Procedure
  • Participants watched a video of two teams passing a basketball
  • Asked to count passes of one team (easy or hard task)
  • Unexpected event: a person in a gorilla suit OR umbrella woman walked through the scene
  • After video: asked if they noticed anything unusual
📊 Results
  • Only 54% noticed the gorilla under easy conditions
  • Hard task: only ~8% noticed unexpected events
  • Gorilla noticed more than umbrella woman (more distinctive)
  • Overall ~46% failed to notice the gorilla
💡 Conclusion
  • Attention is highly selective — unexpected stimuli go unnoticed even when visible
  • Task difficulty and similarity to attended objects affects noticing
  • We are less aware of our environment than we think
✓ Strengths
  • Multiple conditions increase validity of findings
  • High mundane realism — video counting task is plausible
  • Replicated widely — robust and reliable findings
✗ Weaknesses
  • Lab/video task — not identical to real-world divided attention
  • Student sample — low population validity
  • Demand characteristics — participants may have been suspicious
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Usefulness: road safety, aviation, radiology (missed tumours on scans)
  • Individual vs situational: task demands (situational) determine what is noticed

Developmental Area

4 studies · 2 themes · Children's behaviour & moral development

Bandura et al. (1961) — Bobo doll / Transmission of aggression
DevelopmentalClassicTheme: External influences on children's behaviour
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether children learn aggressive behaviour through observation and imitation of an adult model (social learning theory)
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment — matched participants design
  • IV: aggressive vs non-aggressive model; same vs opposite sex model
  • DV: number of aggressive acts directed at Bobo doll
👥 Sample
  • 72 children (36 boys, 36 girls)
  • Aged 3–6 years from Stanford University nursery school
  • Opportunity sample — matched on pre-existing aggression
📋 Procedure
  • Stage 1: Adult model played with toys including Bobo doll
  • Aggressive: hit/kicked Bobo, used verbal aggression ("Sock him!")
  • Non-aggressive: played quietly, ignored Bobo
  • Stage 2: Child mildly frustrated — taken away from attractive toys
  • Stage 3: Child placed in room with Bobo; observed through one-way mirror
📊 Results
  • Aggressive model children showed significantly more aggression
  • Boys showed more physical aggression than girls
  • Children more likely to imitate same-sex models
  • Both physical AND verbal aggression were imitated
💡 Conclusion
  • Aggression is learned through observation and imitation — supports SLT
  • Children do not need direct reinforcement; observation is sufficient
  • Implications for media violence effects on children
✓ Strengths
  • Well-controlled lab — matched participants design controls for individual differences
  • Large sample for a child study — 72 children
  • Foundation of Social Learning Theory and media effects research
✗ Weaknesses
  • Low ecological validity — Bobo doll designed to be hit; not real aggression
  • Demand characteristics — children may have thought they should hit the doll
  • Ethical: deliberate frustration of children; exposure to aggression
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Nature vs nurture: nurture — aggression is learned not innate
  • Socially sensitive: findings used in media censorship debates
Chaney et al. (2004) — Funhaler study
DevelopmentalContemporaryTheme: External influences on children's behaviour
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether the Funhaler (gamified asthma spacer) improves medication compliance in young children vs standard spacer
📐 Design
  • Quasi-experiment — cross-over within-participants design
  • Standard spacer first (2 weeks) → Funhaler (2 weeks)
  • DV: compliance rates reported by parents via questionnaire
👥 Sample
  • 32 asthmatic children aged 1.5–6 years
  • Recruited from a medical centre in Western Australia
  • Opportunity/volunteer sample
📋 Procedure
  • Phase 1: 2 weeks using standard asthma spacer
  • Phase 2: Funhaler — spinning disk and whistle activated on correct inhale
  • Parents completed questionnaires on compliance in each phase
📊 Results
  • 38% more parents reported medicating child on previous day with Funhaler
  • 60% more children took the recommended number of doses
  • Parents found Funhaler easier; children more willing to use it
💡 Conclusion
  • Positive reinforcement built into medical devices improves compliance
  • Behaviourist principles (operant conditioning) applicable to medical contexts
✓ Strengths
  • Directly addresses a real health problem — high applied value
  • Within-participants design reduces individual difference confounds
  • Parent-reported real home behaviour — ecological validity
✗ Weaknesses
  • Very small pilot study (n=32) — cannot generalise
  • Order effect — Funhaler always second; not counterbalanced
  • Self-report from parents — social desirability bias
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Behaviourist perspective: operant conditioning — reward for correct inhaler technique
  • Usefulness: direct medical application — potentially life-saving
Kohlberg (1968) — Stages of moral development
DevelopmentalClassicTheme: Moral development
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate how moral reasoning develops across childhood and into adulthood and identify universal stages of moral development
📐 Design
  • Longitudinal semi-structured interview study
  • Qualitative data — responses analysed for stage of moral reasoning
👥 Sample
  • 72 boys aged 10, 13, 16 from Chicago; later followed to age 36
  • All male, all American — opportunity sample
📋 Procedure
  • Boys presented with moral dilemmas (e.g. Heinz dilemma)
  • Open-ended questions: what should happen? why?
  • Responses assigned to one of six stages across three levels
  • Same boys followed up longitudinally
📊 Results
  • Level 1 Pre-conventional (Stages 1&2): punishment avoidance, self-interest
  • Level 2 Conventional (Stages 3&4): maintaining social order
  • Level 3 Post-conventional (Stages 5&6): universal ethical principles — rare
  • Stages were sequential — no skipping
💡 Conclusion
  • Moral reasoning develops in fixed universal stages
  • Quality of justification matters more than the moral decision itself
✓ Strengths
  • Longitudinal — tracks genuine developmental change
  • Rich qualitative data — reveals reasoning processes
✗ Weaknesses
  • Androcentric — boys only; Gilligan's critique of care-based morality
  • Ethnocentric — universality claims based on American boys only
  • Artificial dilemmas — Heinz is extreme and hypothetical
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Ethnocentrism: Lee et al. directly challenges universal claims
  • Nature vs nurture: universal innate stages (nature) — Lee challenges with culture (nurture)
Lee et al. (1997) — Evaluations of lying and truth-telling
DevelopmentalContemporaryTheme: Moral development
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether Chinese and Canadian children evaluate lying and truth-telling differently, especially for pro-social vs anti-social behaviour
📐 Design
  • Cross-cultural — independent measures (Chinese vs Canadian)
  • Three age groups: 7, 9, and 11
  • Self-report rating scale — ordinal data
👥 Sample
  • Children from China (mainland) and Canada
  • Ages 7, 9, 11 — school-based samples
📋 Procedure
  • Short stories: character told truth or lied in pro-social or anti-social contexts
  • Children rated: was the character good or bad? (on a scale)
  • Same stories used in both countries with equivalent translations
📊 Results
  • Canadian: truth-telling always positive; lying always negative
  • Chinese: lying to conceal pro-social behaviour (modesty) rated positively
  • Truth-telling about good deeds rated negatively by Chinese children (seen as boasting)
  • Cultural difference more pronounced with age
💡 Conclusion
  • Moral evaluations of lying are culturally relative, not universal
  • Challenges Kohlberg's universal stages — culture shapes moral development
✓ Strengths
  • Cross-cultural — directly tests universality of Kohlberg
  • Challenges ethnocentrism in psychology
✗ Weaknesses
  • Story-based — hypothetical, may not reflect real behaviour
  • Social desirability — children may give culturally "correct" answers
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Ethnocentrism: key study showing Western morality not universal benchmark
  • Nature vs nurture: strongly supports nurture — culture shapes moral reasoning

Biological Area

4 studies · 2 themes · Brain regions & plasticity

Sperry (1968) — Split brain study
BiologicalClassicTheme: Regions of the brain
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate separate functions of left and right hemispheres after surgical severing of the corpus callosum
📐 Design
  • Quasi-experiment; IV not manipulated by researcher
  • Repeated measures within each patient for different stimuli
👥 Sample
  • 11 patients who had corpus callosotomy for severe epilepsy
  • All had pre-existing epilepsy — atypical sample
📋 Procedure
  • Visual info presented to left OR right visual field
  • Stimuli flashed for <0.1s to prevent eye movement
  • Tasks: name object (verbal), pick out by touch, draw it
📊 Results
  • Left visual field (right hemisphere): could not name object verbally, but could identify by touch with left hand
  • Right visual field (left hemisphere): could name object verbally
  • Two hemispheres operate independently when corpus callosum severed
💡 Conclusion
  • Left hemisphere: language; right hemisphere: spatial/tactile tasks
  • The two hemispheres are functionally specialised
  • Corpus callosum vital for integrating the hemispheres
✓ Strengths
  • Unique access to a rare condition — high scientific value
  • Multiple tasks across multiple patients — convergent validity
✗ Weaknesses
  • Very small atypical sample — cannot generalise to neurotypical population
  • Pre-existing epilepsy may affect brain organisation
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Nature: biological basis of hemisphere specialisation
  • Reductionism: reduces complex human cognition to hemisphere functions
Casey et al. (2011) — Marshmallow test / Self-control and the brain
BiologicalContemporaryTheme: Regions of the brain
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate the neural correlates of self-control — specifically whether low-delayers in childhood show different brain activity as adults
📐 Design
  • Longitudinal quasi-experiment; fMRI scanning
  • IV: high delayer vs low delayer (based on childhood marshmallow test)
👥 Sample
  • Originally ~562 4-year-olds in marshmallow study (Mischel, 1960s)
  • Follow-up: 59 adults aged ~40 (high and low delayers)
📋 Procedure
  • Adults shown faces with happy, fearful, and neutral expressions
  • Hot task: respond to happy faces only (go/no-go paradigm)
  • fMRI scanned brain activity during the task
📊 Results
  • Low delayers: greater activation in ventral striatum (reward region) during hot task
  • High delayers: greater activation in inferior frontal gyrus (inhibitory control)
  • Behavioural differences: low delayers slower/less accurate at suppressing responses
💡 Conclusion
  • Self-control ability is stable from childhood to adulthood
  • Low self-control is associated with greater reactivity in reward regions and less prefrontal inhibition
  • Biological basis for self-control in prefrontal/striatal circuitry
✓ Strengths
  • Longitudinal — rare and valuable long-term data
  • Objective fMRI measure — avoids self-report bias
✗ Weaknesses
  • Small follow-up sample — original attrition
  • Correlation not causation — brain differences may have other explanations
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Nature vs nurture: biological basis of stable self-control trait
  • Determinism: implies self-control is biologically determined from childhood
Blakemore & Cooper (1970) — Visual cortex plasticity in kittens
BiologicalClassicTheme: Brain plasticity
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether early visual experience shapes the organisation of the visual cortex — testing neural plasticity in the critical period
📐 Design
  • Lab experiment (animal) — independent measures
  • IV: reared in horizontal-only vs vertical-only stripe environment
👥 Sample
  • Kittens raised in cylindrical chambers from birth to 5 months
  • Only exposed to one orientation of stripes (horizontal or vertical)
📋 Procedure
  • Kittens wore neck ruffs to prevent seeing own body
  • After 5 months, normal visual environment
  • Later: single-cell recordings from visual cortex neurons
  • Behavioural tests: walked into horizontal/vertical obstacles
📊 Results
  • Visual cortex neurons responded only to the experienced orientation
  • Horizontal-reared: cortex had no vertical-responsive neurons (and vice versa)
  • Behaviourally: kittens were blind to the unexperienced orientation
💡 Conclusion
  • Brain is highly plastic during the critical period — early experience shapes neural architecture
  • Without appropriate experience, neurons for certain stimuli fail to develop
✓ Strengths
  • High experimental control — IV tightly controlled from birth
  • Objective neurophysiological measures (electrode recordings)
✗ Weaknesses
  • Animal study — cannot directly generalise to humans
  • Severe ethical concerns — kittens raised in impoverished conditions; permanently disabled
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Nature vs nurture: environment shapes biological neural architecture — strong nurture evidence
  • Ethics: animal experimentation debate — knowledge vs welfare
Maguire et al. (2000) — London taxi drivers' hippocampus
BiologicalContemporaryTheme: Brain plasticity
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether the hippocampi of experienced taxi drivers differ structurally from non-taxi drivers, and whether differences relate to navigation experience
📐 Design
  • Quasi-experiment — MRI brain scanning
  • IV: taxi driver vs non-taxi driver
  • Also: correlation between years of experience and hippocampal volume
👥 Sample
  • 16 male London taxi drivers; mean 14.4 years experience
  • 50 male controls (non-taxi drivers)
  • All right-handed, no neurological conditions
📋 Procedure
  • MRI scans of all participants' brains
  • VBM (Voxel-Based Morphometry) to compare grey matter volume
  • Pixel counting of hippocampal regions
📊 Results
  • Taxi drivers had significantly larger posterior hippocampi than controls
  • Controls had larger anterior hippocampi
  • Positive correlation between years of experience and posterior hippocampal volume
💡 Conclusion
  • Human brain remains plastic into adulthood — navigation training enlarges hippocampus
  • Posterior hippocampus involved in spatial navigation and memory
  • Brain structure changes in response to environmental demands
✓ Strengths
  • Objective brain scan measures — no self-report bias
  • Real-world expertise — high ecological validity compared to Blakemore
✗ Weaknesses
  • Correlation cannot establish causation — large hippocampus may have led people to become taxi drivers
  • All male — cannot generalise to female population
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Nature vs nurture: experience reshapes biological brain structures — nurture alters nature
  • Determinism: correlation — does biology determine career choice or vice versa?

Individual Differences Area

4 studies · 2 themes · Disorders & measuring differences

Freud (1909) — Little Hans
Ind diffClassicTheme: Understanding disorders
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To document and provide a psychoanalytic explanation for a young boy's phobia of horses
  • To support the Oedipus complex theory and psychoanalytic interpretation of childhood anxiety
📐 Design
  • Case study — qualitative longitudinal
  • Freud analysed letters written by Hans's father
  • Single participant — idiographic approach
👥 Sample
  • One participant — Herbert ("Hans"), aged ~3–5
  • Son of a Viennese doctor; middle class family
  • Opportunity — Hans's father was a follower of Freud
📋 Procedure
  • Hans's father wrote detailed accounts of Hans's behaviour and conversations
  • Freud provided interpretations via letters; met Hans only once
  • Data: Hans's dreams, fantasies, questions, and phobic responses analysed
📊 Results
  • Hans developed fear of horses — especially being bitten
  • Freud interpreted horses as symbol of his feared father
  • Fear linked to castration anxiety and unresolved Oedipus complex
  • Phobia resolved by end of study (Hans matured through complex)
💡 Conclusion
  • Phobias have unconscious symbolic origins — not purely behavioural
  • Oedipus complex is central to childhood anxiety
  • Psychoanalytic interpretation can explain and treat childhood phobias
✓ Strengths
  • Rich in-depth data — detailed picture of child's inner world
  • One of first accounts of childhood psychological disorder
✗ Weaknesses
  • Highly subjective — Freud interpreted data through pre-existing theory
  • Cannot generalise — single atypical case
  • Freud never directly observed Hans — relied on father's (biased) accounts
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Psychodynamic perspective: unconscious drives, symbolic interpretation
  • Determinism: early childhood experiences determine adult personality
Baron-Cohen et al. (1997) — Eyes Test / Theory of Mind in autism
Ind diffContemporaryTheme: Understanding disorders
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether high-functioning adults with autism or Asperger's syndrome have a Theory of Mind deficit, using a more sensitive test than previous tasks
📐 Design
  • Quasi-experiment — independent measures
  • Three groups: autism/AS, Tourette syndrome, neurotypical controls
👥 Sample
  • 16 adults with autism/Asperger's syndrome; 50 neurotypical adults; 10 adults with Tourette syndrome
  • Opportunity sample from clinical records and community
📋 Procedure
  • Eyes Test: 25 photographs of eyes — choose mental state word from pair of options
  • Also: gender recognition task (control); strange stories task (mentalising)
📊 Results
  • Autism/AS: mean 16.3/25 on Eyes Test; controls: 20.3/25; Tourette: 20.4/25
  • All groups performed equally on gender recognition (control task)
  • Women scored significantly higher than men on Eyes Test
💡 Conclusion
  • Adults with autism/AS have a specific ToM deficit — not general cognitive impairment
  • Even high-functioning autistic individuals struggle to read mental states from eyes
  • Supports "mindblindness" theory of autism
✓ Strengths
  • More sensitive than Sally-Anne false belief task — catches high-functioning adults
  • Control task rules out general perceptual or cognitive impairment
✗ Weaknesses
  • Quasi-experiment — cannot manipulate IV; potential selection bias
  • Photographs are decontextualised — lacks ecological validity
  • Small autism sample (n=16)
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Individual differences: cognitive deficit distinguishes autistic from neurotypical
  • Nature: ToM deficit suggests biological/neurological basis for autism
Gould (1982) — A nation of morons / Army intelligence testing
Ind diffClassicTheme: Measuring differences
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To critically analyse the Army Alpha and Beta IQ testing programmes of WWI and evaluate the claims made from the data
📐 Design
  • Secondary data analysis — critique of existing data
  • Qualitative and quantitative analysis of published test data and social policy
👥 Sample
  • Gould analysed data from ~1.75 million WWI Army recruits
  • Tests administered by Yerkes and colleagues 1917–1919
📋 Procedure
  • Army Alpha: written tests for literate recruits
  • Army Beta: non-verbal tests for illiterate/non-English speakers
  • Gould critically examined: test design, administration conditions, data interpretation, and social consequences
📊 Results / Findings
  • Tests were culturally biased — assumed American cultural knowledge
  • Testing conditions were appalling — crowded, no equipment
  • Average mental age of recruits found to be ~13 (flawed methodology)
  • Results used to justify racist immigration restrictions (1924 Immigration Act)
💡 Conclusion
  • IQ testing in WWI was methodologically flawed and culturally biased
  • Data was misused to justify racist social policy
  • Intelligence testing has a dangerous history of serving prejudiced agendas
✓ Strengths
  • Exposes real-world harm from pseudoscience
  • Secondary analysis allows evaluation of data that would be unethical to collect today
✗ Weaknesses
  • Secondary source — Gould did not collect data himself
  • Gould's own analysis has been criticised for selective reading of evidence
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Ethnocentrism: central example of culturally biased measurement tools
  • Socially sensitive: research used to justify discrimination and genocide
Hancock et al. (2011) — Language and psychopathy
Ind diffContemporaryTheme: Measuring differences
▼
🎯 Aim
  • To investigate whether psychopathic murderers use different language patterns in murder narratives than non-psychopathic murderers
📐 Design
  • Quasi-experiment — content analysis
  • IV: psychopathic vs non-psychopathic (based on PCL-R scores)
  • Computer-based linguistic analysis (LIWC)
👥 Sample
  • 52 male murderers in Canadian prisons
  • 14 scored ≥30 on PCL-R (psychopathic); 38 non-psychopathic
📋 Procedure
  • Each participant recorded an account of their crime
  • Transcripts analysed using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC)
  • PCL-R psychopathy scores obtained from prison records
📊 Results
  • Psychopaths used more past tense, fewer future/present tense words
  • More cause-and-effect language ("because", "so that")
  • More material (food, money, need) and fewer emotional words
  • More disfluencies (uh, um) — suggests greater cognitive effort
💡 Conclusion
  • Psychopaths perceive murder as a means to an end (pragmatic, goal-driven)
  • Murder is a past-tense, completed goal — emotionally detached language
  • Language analysis could assist in identifying psychopathy
✓ Strengths
  • Objective computerised analysis — no researcher interpretation bias
  • Novel method (content analysis) for measuring individual differences in cognition
✗ Weaknesses
  • All male, Canadian prisoners — very low generalisability
  • Small psychopathic group (n=14)
  • Demand characteristics — prisoners may have crafted their accounts strategically
🔗 Debate/area links
  • Individual differences: psychopathy as measurable cognitive/linguistic trait
  • Usefulness: forensic application — offender profiling and risk assessment

Areas & Perspectives

Five areas · Key assumptions · Study links

Social Area
▼

Core assumptions

  • Behaviour is determined by the social context we are in
  • Situational factors outweigh dispositional factors
  • Groups, authority, and conformity shape behaviour

Strengths as an area

  • High ecological validity (Piliavin field experiment)
  • Directly applicable to real-world issues (obedience, bystander)
  • Cross-cultural replication (Levine, Bocchiaro)

Weaknesses as an area

  • Ethical issues — deception (Milgram, Piliavin)
  • May underestimate dispositional factors
  • Lab studies lack mundane realism (Milgram, Bocchiaro)
Cognitive Area
▼

Core assumptions

  • Mental processes (thinking, memory, attention) can be studied scientifically
  • Brain processes information like a computer
  • Behaviour is mediated by mental representation

Strengths

  • Highly applicable (Loftus → cognitive interview; Grant → revision)
  • Scientific methods — controlled experiments
  • Moved psychology beyond behaviourism

Weaknesses

  • Lab tasks lack ecological validity
  • Computer metaphor oversimplifies human cognition
  • Ignores emotional and social context

Issues & Debates

Key OCR debates with study examples

Nature vs Nurture
▼

Nature side

  • Sperry — hemisphere specialisation is biological
  • Casey et al. — self-control linked to brain structure
  • Baron-Cohen — ToM deficit as biological feature of autism
  • Kohlberg — universal innate moral stages

Nurture side

  • Bandura — aggression learned through observation
  • Blakemore & Cooper — environment shapes visual cortex
  • Maguire — navigation experience enlarges hippocampus
  • Lee et al. — culture shapes moral judgements
  • Loftus & Palmer — post-event language alters memory
Individual vs Situational Explanations
▼

Individual

  • Baron-Cohen — specific cognitive deficit in autism
  • Freud — unconscious individual drives
  • Casey et al. — stable individual self-control trait
  • Hancock — psychopathic personality traits

Situational

  • Milgram — authority situation determined obedience
  • Piliavin — physical context (trapped subway) affected helping
  • Simons & Chabris — task demands determined attention
  • Bocchiaro — situation drives compliance/whistleblowing
Ethnocentrism
▼

Ethnocentric studies

  • Milgram — American male participants only
  • Kohlberg — American boys as universal baseline
  • Loftus & Palmer — American students
  • Gould — tests designed with American cultural assumptions

Challenges / solutions

  • Bocchiaro — Netherlands replication of Milgram
  • Lee et al. — cross-cultural challenge to Kohlberg
  • Levine — 23 cities across cultures

Exam Technique — Paper 2

Based on examiner reports and mark scheme patterns from past papers

Section A — Core study questions
"Outline the procedure" [2–4 marks]State WHAT happened in order — who did what, in what conditions, what was measured. Include specific details (e.g. numbers, conditions). Do NOT include results or conclusions. Cover all key steps — missing key steps loses marks.
"Outline one finding" [2 marks]State a specific result with a number/statistic where possible. Then briefly explain what this shows. E.g. "65% of participants administered the maximum 450V shock, suggesting that most people will obey authority figures even when asked to harm others."
"Outline one strength AND one weakness" [4 marks]For each: State the point → Explain WHY it is a strength/weakness in the context of THIS study → Link to a methodological concept (validity, reliability, ethics, generalisability). 2 marks per point = state + explain.
"Explain one validity issue" [3 marks]Name the type of validity → Explain what it means → Apply specifically to this study. E.g. "Bandura's study has low ecological validity because the Bobo doll is specifically designed to be hit, so children may not have been showing real-world aggression."
"Compare the two paired studies" [4 marks]Give one clear similarity AND one clear difference — or two of either. Always specify BOTH studies by name. Show you understand how the contemporary study updates/extends the classic.
"How does [study] relate to [area]?" [3–5 marks]State the defining principles of the area → Link the study's aim/findings to those principles → Explain how the study exemplifies or contributes to that area.
Section B — Areas, perspectives, debates
15-mark comparison essay — top tipsUse studies illustratively, not descriptively. Compare at the LEVEL OF THE AREA. Draw on debates (ethics, validity, scientific status). Reach a clear judgement in your conclusion.
Debate essay structurePara 1: Define both sides. Para 2: Studies supporting A. Para 3: Studies supporting B. Para 4: Nuanced/interactionist view. Para 5: Conclusion with judgement. Always use findings as evidence, never just names.
